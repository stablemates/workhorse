---
title: Multi-tenancy
description: Run many tenants on Workhorse, with one database each or one shared database whose tasks carry the tenant.
---

A tenant is a customer, team, or account whose work must stay fair and bounded. Sometimes the work
of a tenant must also stay apart from the work of other tenants. Workhorse gives two ways to do
this, and they differ in who enforces the boundary between tenants.

- With **shared tenancy**, all tenants use one database. Each task carries the identifier of its
  tenant, and your app keeps that identifier consistent.
- With **isolated tenancy**, each tenant has its own database. PostgreSQL enforces the boundary.

Use shared tenancy if many tenants need a fair share and limits for each tenant. Use isolated
tenancy if the rows of one tenant must not be in the same tables as the rows of another tenant.

## Give each tenant a fair share of one database

**Example.** Queue `reports` serves all customers in one database. The concurrency policy lets the
queue run 40 reports at the same time, and a maximum of 4 for each tenant. The budget of each tenant
lets it run a maximum of 8 tasks across all queues.

1. At 09:00, tenant `acme` enqueues 60 monthly reports. Four reports start, and 56 reports wait. The
   other 36 slots stay free, because `acme` uses all of its 4 slots.
2. At 09:01, tenant `globex` enqueues 3 reports. The claim skips the waiting `acme` reports, and the
   3 `globex` reports start immediately.
3. At 09:02, `acme` enqueues exports on queue `exports`. Its budget, also named `acme`, counts its 4
   reports too. Four exports start, and the other exports wait.
4. At 09:05, an operator filters the dashboard task list by the tag `tenant:acme`. The list shows
   only the work of `acme`.

In a shared database, the tenant is not a column. Your app writes the same tenant identifier into
three enqueue options. Workhorse already enforces each of these options:

- `concurrencyKey` gives each tenant a fair share in a queue. A concurrency policy with
  `maxActivePerKey` sets the maximum number of active tasks for one tenant. A rate policy with
  `perKey` gives each tenant its own token bucket. See [concurrency policies](/docs/concurrency-policies) and
  [rate limits](/docs/rate-limits).
- `budget` sets a limit for one tenant across all queues. Synchronize one budget for each tenant
  with `Queue.syncBudgets`. Each task names the budget of its tenant.
- A `tenant:` tag lets an operator filter by tenant. Dead-letter lists, redrive filters, and the
  dashboard task list accept a tag filter.

In Go, set `ConcurrencyKey`, `Budget`, and `Tags` on `EnqueueOptions`, and synchronize with
`Queue.SyncBudgets`. In Python, set `concurrency_key`, `budget`, and `tags`, and synchronize with
`Queue.sync_budgets` or `AsyncQueue.sync_budgets`. In Rust, set the same snake_case fields, and synchronize with
`Queue::sync_budgets`. In Ruby, give the same names as keywords, and synchronize with
`Queue#sync_budgets`.

If a tenant is at its limit, the claim skips the tasks of that tenant. The claim examines only a
limited window of the first ready tasks. If the waiting tasks of one tenant fill that whole window,
the claim finds no task of another tenant.
[Keep a full key from blocking other work](/docs/concurrency-policies#keep-a-full-key-from-blocking-other-work)
tells how to prevent this.

Do not make a queue for each tenant. A queue groups work for dispatch, not tenants. Many tenants
share one queue, and the key and the budget keep the work fair. A queue for each tenant can work for
a small number of tenants. But each new queue adds health rows, worker queue lists, and policy rows,
and it adds no isolation.

A recurring schedule names its tenant with the `concurrencyKey` in its task definition. Schedule
definitions do not carry tags or a budget. Thus, a scheduled task gets a fair share in its queue,
but no limit across queues. If the handler enqueues more work, give that work all three options.

<details>
<summary>Reference: the three tenant fields</summary>

| Field                                    | Bounds                                    | Role                                                                                                |
| ---------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `task.concurrency_key`                   | 1 to 256 UTF-8 bytes, queue-scoped        | `concurrency_policy.max_active_per_key` and the `rate_limit_policy` per-key bucket give fair share. |
| `task.budget_name`                       | 1 to 256 UTF-8 bytes                      | One `budget` row per tenant caps that tenant's active tasks or start rate across every queue.       |
| One `tenant:<id>` element of `task.tags` | At most 20 tags, each 1 to 100 characters | `task_tags_gin_idx` serves the tag filters.                                                         |

**Tag filters**

| Read                                                                           | Match                     |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------- |
| `list_dead_letters_v1` and `redrive_many_v1`, through `DeadLetterFilter.tags`  | Every supplied tag (`@>`) |
| `dashboard_tasks_v1`, `dashboard_tasks_cursor_v1`, and `dashboard_activity_v1` | Any selected tag (`&&`)   |

- The dashboard wire validator accepts at most 20 selected tags.
- `Admin.listTasks` has no tag filter.
- The claim inspects at most the first 100 ready rows of the queue, in priority, sequence, and
  task-identity order. The rows of a saturated key stay in that window.
- `sync_budgets_v1` accepts at most 10,000 definitions per call. On the benchmark host, one budget
  per tenant costs about 130 microseconds per tenant to synchronize.
- `schedule_definition` keeps `concurrency_key` and carries neither tags nor a budget.
- `namespace` on schedules, policies, and budgets names the owning deployment, not a tenant.

More detail: [Overview: Shared tenancy](https://github.com/stablemates/workhorse/blob/main/docs/architecture/overview.md#shared-tenancy).

</details>

## Keep each tenant in its own database

**Example.** A contract with `acme` says that its records must not share a table with the records of
another customer. Thus, `acme` and `globex` each get a database with the Workhorse schema.

1. At setup, you install the schema in the `acme` database and in the `globex` database.
2. At run time, one worker fleet connects to `acme`, and a second fleet connects to `globex`. Each
   worker connects to one database. Thus, no worker sees the tasks of both tenants.
3. At each deployment, you run `workhorse schema migrate` two times, one time for each database.
   Each database upgrades independently.

The databases are separate. Thus, the tasks, queues, schedules, policies, budgets, retention, and
dashboard of each tenant are separate. The schema does not need to know about tenants.

This model has an operational cost. Each new tenant adds a schema upgrade to each deployment. It
also adds a worker fleet that holds its own connections. Telemetry is also separate. The process
resource attributes of each fleet identify its tenant.

<details>
<summary>Reference: isolated tenancy</summary>

- PostgreSQL enforces the boundary between databases.
- Every table, policy, budget, schedule, retention window, and dashboard session is separate.
- Each database runs `workhorse schema migrate` independently.
- Each `Worker` binds to one database.
- In isolated tenancy, the process resource attributes identify the tenant, one worker fleet at a
  time.

More detail: [Overview: Isolated tenancy](https://github.com/stablemates/workhorse/blob/main/docs/architecture/overview.md#isolated-tenancy).

</details>

## What shared tenancy does not do

Shared tenancy keeps work fair. It does not make a security boundary.

**Example.** Your app enqueues a task with `concurrencyKey: "acme"`. A defect in the app writes the
tag `tenant:globex` on the same task.

1. Workhorse accepts the task.
2. Workhorse counts the task against the fair share of `acme`.
3. An operator filters by `tenant:globex` and sees the task in the list of `globex`.

Your app must keep the key, the tag, and the budget of a task consistent. Workhorse does not reject
a task whose key, tag, and budget do not agree.

Retention applies to the full installation. The history of all tenants uses the same retention
windows. If a tenant needs different windows, give it its own database.

Operator access applies to all tenants. An administrator sees the tasks of all tenants. An
authorized cancel, redrive, or purge can change any task. A tag filter makes a view smaller, but it
does not enforce a boundary. Workhorse does not yet limit operator access to one tenant. The
[limitations](/docs/limitations) page records this limit.

Metrics do not carry the tenant. The queue and the task type limit the number of series for each
counter and histogram. A tag or key attribute would make the number of series grow with the number
of tenants. If a handler must show the tenant on its own spans, it reads the tenant from the
payload.

<details>
<summary>Reference: what shared tenancy does not scope</summary>

- No check compares the `concurrency_key`, `budget_name`, and tags of a task.
- `retention_policy` is a singleton, so retention windows are per installation.
- No function scopes a read or an operator action to a tenant.
- Metric attributes never include a key or tag.
- Spans carry `workhorse.task.id` and `workhorse.task.type`, not the key or tags.
- [`docs/features.md`](https://github.com/stablemates/workhorse/blob/main/docs/features.md) records
  that tenant-scoped operator access is not supported.

More detail: [Overview: Shared tenancy](https://github.com/stablemates/workhorse/blob/main/docs/architecture/overview.md#shared-tenancy).

</details>

## Add tenants without slowing the claim

The cost to claim one task does not increase with the number of tenants. Each count that the claim
does reads only the rows of one tenant.

**Example.** Queue `reports` holds work for 100,000 tenants. The 40 tenants at the head of the queue
are all at their limit.

1. A worker claims a task.
2. Workhorse counts the active tasks of each tenant at the head. A partial index on the key lets
   each count read only the rows of that tenant.
3. The claim skips the tasks of the full tenants, in a limited window of ready tasks.
4. The claim starts the first task whose tenant has a free slot.

Workhorse counts the active tasks of a budget in the same way, with a partial index on the budget
name. Thus, the claim of one task never reads the rows of other tenants. The tenant cardinality
benchmark loads one queue with more and more tenants. The claim latency stays almost the same at
each step.

<details>
<summary>Reference: admission cost and benchmark</summary>

- `claim_v1` counts one key through `task_runtime_active_queue_key_expiry_idx`.
- It counts one budget through `task_runtime_active_budget_expiry_idx`.
- When a per-key limit or budget passes over saturated work, the claim inspects at most 100 ready
  rows.

`pnpm benchmark:tenant-cardinality` loads one queue with 100 through 100,000 tenants. Each tenant
has a key, a tag, and optionally its own budget. The queue has 80 saturated rows at the head of the
window.

| Measurement                        | Across the ladder                                               |
| ---------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Median claim latency, no budgets   | 2.2 to 3.5 ms                                                   |
| Median claim latency, with budgets | 3.7 to 4.4 ms                                                   |
| One admission count                | Three buffer hits                                               |
| Dashboard task list by tag         | 1 ms to 94 ms. It tracks total rows, not tenants.               |
| `queue_health_v1`                  | 2.7 s at 200,000 ready rows. It tracks total rows, not tenants. |

The repository's benchmark analysis for 2026-09-16 records the full figures.

More detail: [Overview: Admission cost](https://github.com/stablemates/workhorse/blob/main/docs/architecture/overview.md#admission-cost).

</details>
